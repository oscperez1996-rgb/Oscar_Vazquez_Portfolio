ES 51 Computer-Aided Machine Design · Spring 2025 · Best CAD Award

GlitterBot

An all-wheel-drive competition robot with an extending four-bar claw. Our team won the Turf Wars award for best CAD design.

AwardBest CAD design, Turf Wars 2025
My roleDrawings + figures · analysis + calculations
TeamFive students

01 / The brief

Climb, grab, and score fast.

Turf Wars is the final project for ES 51, Harvard's intro to mechanical design. Teams design and build a remote-controlled robot from a limited kit, then compete in a double-elimination tournament of four-minute matches. That year's game was tic-tac-toe. Robots crossed turf and climbed 15° and 30° ramps to collect blocks, pucks and dog toys and place them on a grid. Every robot also had to start inside the 11 × 11 × 11 in "Box of Justice."

We set three targets of our own: all-wheel drive for every surface, a claw that opens at least an inch wider than the largest game piece, and a top speed of at least 5 in/s.

02 / Concepts

Three concepts, one scoring matrix.

We compared three directions. The basic robot used a single-joint arm. The dank robot used raised horizontal slides to reach pieces without climbing. The speedy robot put three motors on one drive shaft. A Pugh matrix scored each against a baseline on collection speed, center of mass, fitting the starting box, reach and maneuverability. We built the winning layout in cardboard to check its proportions.

03 / Analysis

Sizing the drivetrain for the 30° ramp.

Working from the center of mass reported by SolidWorks, we drew a free-body diagram of the robot on the ramp and solved for the wheel force needed to overcome gravity, friction and inertia. From that we found the minimum wheel torque. One motor couldn't meet both the force and speed targets, so we used two, geared down at 4.5:1.

We also tested stability on the real ramp. We loaded weights onto the bare acrylic base and set it on the ramp to find where it would tip. Since the raised claw would be the biggest tipping risk, we kept it low.

04 / CAD

The award-winning model.

The whole robot lived in SolidWorks before we cut anything. Mating the parts showed us problems early, and the model gave us the mass and center of mass for the analysis. It also showed that cutting out the middle of the base plate would free up room for the claw and save weight.

We ran a motion study of the drivetrain climbing a model of the ramp, and placed the full robot in a model of the arena.

05 / Drawings + cut files

From model to shop-ready parts.

Dimensioned drawings turned the model into parts we could make: laser-cut acrylic plates, 3D-printed mounts and pulleys, and steel hex shafts cut to ±0.010 in. After the first drivetrain build we refined the plates. T-slots moved in from the edges so the acrylic wouldn't crack, sharp corners got fillets, and new holes were added for the claw.

06 / Fabrication

Laser cutter, calipers and the turf.

We cut the chassis plates from acrylic on the laser cutter. On the first cut the laser settings warped the ¼" sheet, so we adjusted them for the final run. Shafts were measured, marked and cut by hand, and the drivetrain went together in the shop before its first run on the turf.

07 / The robot

Low, quick, and a claw that reaches out.

Two motors drive the rear wheels, and timing belts carry the power to the front, which gives all-wheel drive on four silicone-cast wheels. The claw is a four-bar linkage gripper. Its arm works like a steam engine's connecting rod: linear motion swings it up and out, so it can reach far past the chassis and still fold down.

Folded, the robot stands 7 in tall, while every other claw design at the competition was fixed at 10 in. That kept our center of gravity low for the ramps. The robot beat the 5 in/s speed target, and the claw opens 4 in, wider than the 3 in dog toy.

09 / What I learned

Testing time is a design requirement.

On competition day the claw worked every time, but the drivetrain didn't. A dead battery, a broken wire and a gear tooth that sheared and walked out of mesh kept us from moving. The claw proved itself, but the drivetrain needed more time on the turf. Next time I'd budget more testing for the drivetrain, pin the gears in place on their hex shafts, and slow down during assembly.
